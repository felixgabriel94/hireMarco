# Two jobs: mixing and mastering

The artist's rule (2026-11-02): mixing and mastering are two separate jobs
with two different intentions, and Marco thinks differently in each. Mixing
is hands-on. Mastering is selective: subtle enhancements to a finished mix.
The way he tackles each role is specified here, and the plan says which role
it is working in before any move.

## Mixing: build the record

- What it works on: the separate tracks, takes and busses of one song
  (R-AUD-0004: "a mixing engineer sculpts and balances the separate tracks").
- The intention: make every part sit where the artist hears it. Balance,
  placement, tone, dynamics and space are decided here, per part and per
  moment; the lead stays the center of the picture and the atmosphere is
  built around it.
- The scale of a move: whatever the part needs. A cut of 6 dB at one note on
  one take is a mixing move; so is a compressor at 4:1 on a vocal.
- The order: fix before you flatter. Remove what bothers (a resonance, a
  rumble, a click, a sibilant hit) per take before any tone shaping; then
  balance; then dynamics; then space; then the bus. A problem solved in the
  mix never reaches the master (master rulebook, the Joshua line).
- The tools: every plug-in the artist owns, to its reach. Dynamic bands
  (R-AUD-0006) belong here: a cut that moves only when the band crosses its
  threshold, so the hit is tamed and the rest of the take is untouched.
- What leaves: a mix with headroom, no limiter on the output, the whole song
  with its tails, and a note of what the bus already does (R-AUD-0004,
  R-AUD-0005).

## Mastering: finish the record

- What it works on: one stereo file, the finished mix (R-AUD-0004: "A
  mastering engineer then listens to the whole piece as a stereo mixdown").
- The intention: the final touches that make the record play right
  everywhere, consistent with the artist's other records and the genre now,
  at the loudness the purpose asks for. "They're thinking about the finished
  product and if there's something they might need to do to improve the
  sound." (R-AUD-0004) "any compression or EQ added by the mastering engineer
  needs to be subtle." (R-AUD-0005) It is not a remix (R-AUD-0005).
- The scale of a move: small and few. Half a dB is a move. A chain is as
  short as the track allows: "Only use as many as you need." (R-AUD-0004)
- The order: audit first, working level first, corrective before sweetening,
  the limiter last (master rulebook). Every insert earns its place on the
  bench, printed with and without it (the artist's rule, 2026-11-02).
- The tools: a transparent EQ, a gentle wideband compressor only when the
  mix asks for glue, a true-peak limiter to the purpose's ceiling, meters
  read on the file (R-AUD-0001, R-AUD-0002, R-AUD-0003, R-AUD-0011).
- What leaves: the master with its purpose named, integrated loudness, true
  peak, loudness range, sample rate and bit depth (master rulebook).

## How the thinking differs

| | Mixing | Mastering |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of work | a part, a take, a moment | the whole song, the whole record |
| Question asked | what does this part need to sit right? | what does this finished mix need to play right everywhere? |
| Size of a move | as large as the part needs | as small as the song allows |
| When in doubt | solve it here, per take | leave it; send the note to the mix |
| Proof | the part in the mix, soloed and in context | the print against the mix, loudness-matched, on the bench |

A plan that applies mastering restraint to a mixing problem leaves the
problem in; a plan that applies mixing force to a master rewrites the mix.
Marco names the role first, then reasons inside it.
